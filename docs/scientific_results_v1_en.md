
# Scientific Results V1 — ESS Round 11 and Eurostat Socioeconomic Indicators

**Status:** FINAL — Step 66

**Empirical basis:** Scientific Evidence Matrix V1, independently validated in Step 65

**Analysis type:** Cross-sectional, exploratory, country-level analysis

## 1. Overview of the Results

The analysis examined 36 associations between six aggregated variables from the European Social Survey (ESS Round 11) and six Eurostat socioeconomic indicators. The statistical unit was the country, and the number of available observations for each association ranged from 25 to 28.

The ESS variables included mean social trust, Left–Right ideological self-placement, and four Schwartz Human Values dimensions: Openness to Change, Conservation, Self-Transcendence, and Self-Enhancement.

The socioeconomic indicators included GDP per capita expressed as an index in purchasing power standards (GDP per capita in PPS, EU average = 100), employment rate, unemployment rate, Gini coefficient, material deprivation rate, and inflation rate.

Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated and complemented by three categories of sensitivity analyses: Leave-One-Country-Out (LOO), changes in the socioeconomic reference year, and, for the Schwartz dimensions, the exclusion of Estonia (EE), the United Kingdom (GB), or both countries.

The interpretation prioritizes coefficient magnitude and direction, Pearson–Spearman agreement, sample size, and sensitivity to the examined analytical modifications.

The results are presented descriptively, without applying arbitrary robustness thresholds or drawing causal conclusions.

## 2. Social Trust and Socioeconomic Indicators

Mean social trust exhibited a positive association with GDP PPS (Pearson = 0.692; Spearman = 0.789; N = 28) and with the employment rate (Pearson = 0.561; Spearman = 0.586; N = 26).

Negative associations were observed for material deprivation (Pearson = −0.574; Spearman = −0.495; N = 26) and income inequality measured by the Gini coefficient (Pearson = −0.431; Spearman = −0.434; N = 25).

For these four relationships, coefficient signs were preserved across all LOO scenarios and the temporal sensitivity analysis.

However, coefficient magnitudes were not entirely stable. For example, in the Social Trust–Gini relationship, the maximum absolute LOO change was approximately 0.149 for Pearson and 0.139 for Spearman.

The relationship with unemployment exhibited smaller negative coefficients (Pearson = −0.223; Spearman = −0.204; N = 26), with signs preserved across the examined LOO and temporal scenarios.

By contrast, the association with inflation was weak in the baseline analysis and exhibited opposite signs for Pearson (−0.059) and Spearman (+0.107; N = 27).

This relationship was sensitive to changes in the economic reference year. The temporal coefficient changes were approximately −0.243 for Pearson and −0.292 for Spearman, with the latter involving a strict sign reversal.

Therefore, the associations between social trust and socioeconomic indicators did not exhibit a uniform degree of descriptive stability.

## 3. Schwartz Human Values and Socioeconomic Indicators

### 3.1. Openness to Change and Conservation

Associations in opposite directions were observed between these two value dimensions and employment.

Openness to Change exhibited a positive association with employment (Pearson = 0.592; Spearman = 0.607; N = 26), whereas Conservation exhibited a negative association (Pearson = −0.674; Spearman = −0.616; N = 26).

Both relationships retained their direction across all applicable LOO, temporal, and EE/GB exclusion scenarios.

The maximum absolute LOO changes for the Openness–Employment relationship were approximately 0.077 for Pearson and 0.061 for Spearman.

For Conservation–Employment, the maximum absolute LOO changes were approximately 0.074 for Pearson and 0.058 for Spearman.

Regarding GDP PPS, Openness to Change exhibited a positive association (Pearson = 0.322; Spearman = 0.441; N = 28), whereas Conservation exhibited a negative association (Pearson = −0.452; Spearman = −0.577; N = 28).

By contrast, the Openness–Unemployment and Openness–Gini relationships exhibited coefficients close to zero and multiple sign reversals in the LOO analyses.

For Openness–Unemployment, the baseline coefficients were approximately −0.007 for both Pearson and Spearman, with each method exhibiting nine LOO sign reversals.

For Openness–Gini, the baseline coefficients were approximately −0.012 for Pearson and −0.027 for Spearman, with eight Pearson and six Spearman sign reversals across the LOO scenarios.

These findings limit the interpretation of the direction of the respective associations.

### 3.2. Self-Transcendence and Self-Enhancement

Self-Transcendence was positively associated with GDP PPS (Pearson = 0.503; Spearman = 0.645; N = 28) and negatively associated with material deprivation (Pearson = −0.521; Spearman = −0.430; N = 26).

Self-Enhancement exhibited associations in the opposite direction for the same indicators: GDP PPS (Pearson = −0.398; Spearman = −0.581; N = 28) and material deprivation (Pearson = 0.442; Spearman = 0.431; N = 26).

These four relationships exhibited no sign reversals in the LOO analyses, temporal analyses, or applicable EE/GB exclusion scenarios.

Nevertheless, variations in coefficient magnitude were observed and must be considered when interpreting the results.

For example, the maximum absolute Pearson change across LOO scenarios was approximately 0.135 for Self-Transcendence–GDP PPS and approximately 0.125 for Self-Enhancement–GDP PPS.

The Schwartz scores analyzed are centered around each respondent's individual mean response and therefore represent relative value priorities.

Consequently, opposing association patterns should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of independent psychological mechanisms.

## 4. Left–Right Ideological Self-Placement

Mean ideological self-placement generally exhibited smaller coefficients than those observed for certain relationships involving social trust or Schwartz Human Values.

The association with the Gini coefficient was positive (Pearson = 0.327; Spearman = 0.275; N = 25).

By contrast, the relationships with employment (Pearson = −0.031; Spearman = −0.064; N = 26) and unemployment (Pearson = 0.029; Spearman = 0.119; N = 26) exhibited coefficients close to zero.

The Left–Right–Employment relationship exhibited three Pearson and two Spearman sign reversals in the LOO analyses.

The Left–Right–Unemployment relationship exhibited five Pearson LOO sign reversals, whereas the Spearman coefficient exhibited no LOO sign reversals.

For inflation, Pearson (+0.086) and Spearman (−0.076; N = 27) exhibited opposite signs, while the temporal analysis identified a Spearman sign reversal.

These findings do not indicate a uniform association between aggregated ideological self-placement and the socioeconomic indicators examined.

They describe only the statistical relationships obtained using the selected operationalizations and samples.

## 5. Sensitivity Analyses and Stability of the Results

### 5.1. Pearson–Spearman Agreement

For 34 of the 36 relationships, Pearson and Spearman coefficients exhibited the same strict sign.

The two exceptions were Social Trust–Inflation and Left–Right–Inflation.

In both cases, baseline coefficient magnitudes were small.

However, agreement in coefficient sign is not a sufficient measure of relationship stability. Differences in magnitude and sensitivity to the selection of observations must be examined separately.

### 5.2. Leave-One-Country-Out

The LOO analysis included 948 recalculations of correlation pairs, obtained by successively excluding each available country for each of the 36 relationships.

Nine relationships exhibited at least one strict Pearson sign reversal, and seven relationships exhibited at least one Spearman sign reversal.

One example is Openness–Unemployment, where the baseline coefficients were very close to zero and each method exhibited nine LOO sign reversals.

Consequently, these reversals do not automatically constitute evidence of substantial associations in opposite directions.

Furthermore, the absence of sign reversals does not imply stability in coefficient magnitude.

For Self-Transcendence–Gini, the maximum absolute Pearson change was approximately 0.189, without a sign reversal.

For Social Trust–Gini, the maximum absolute Pearson change was approximately 0.149, also without a sign reversal.

The ranges obtained through LOO analysis describe sensitivity to the omission of observations and do not represent confidence intervals.

### 5.3. Sensitivity to the Socioeconomic Reference Year

The temporal analysis identified one strict Pearson sign reversal, for Openness–Unemployment, and two strict Spearman sign reversals, for Social Trust–Inflation and Left–Right–Inflation.

The largest absolute changes were observed in relationships involving inflation.

For Self-Enhancement–Inflation, the coefficient changes were approximately +0.296 for Pearson and +0.455 for Spearman.

For Self-Transcendence–Inflation, the changes were approximately −0.268 for Pearson and −0.356 for Spearman.

For Social Trust–Inflation, the changes were approximately −0.243 for Pearson and −0.292 for Spearman.

These findings demonstrate that the interpretation of certain associations depends on the selection of the socioeconomic reference year.

Temporal comparisons were conducted using common-country samples to distinguish changes in the reference year from changes in sample composition.

For Gini, the common-country sample was smaller than the baseline sample, decreasing from 25 to 24 countries because one observation was unavailable in the alternative temporal scenario.

### 5.4. Sensitivity to the Exclusion of EE and GB

For the 24 Schwartz–Eurostat relationships, three additional scenarios were evaluated: excluding EE, excluding GB, and excluding both EE and GB simultaneously.

None of these scenarios produced strict Pearson or Spearman sign reversals.

The maximum absolute Pearson changes were 0.031088 for the exclusion of EE, 0.006686 for GB, and 0.033706 for EE+GB.

The corresponding maximum Spearman changes were 0.081344, 0.022082, and 0.081344.

Estonia was present in all 24 baseline Schwartz samples.

The United Kingdom was present in only the four Schwartz samples corresponding to GDP PPS. For the other five socioeconomic indicators, excluding GB did not change the analyzed sample.

Consequently, the absence of changes in the `exclude_GB` scenario for these five indicators does not constitute additional evidence of stability; it results from the country's absence from the original sample.

These checks describe sensitivity to specific country exclusions and do not constitute tests of psychometric measurement invariance.

## 6. Limitations of Interpretation

The analysis is cross-sectional and exploratory, and the statistical unit is the country. Therefore, aggregate correlations cannot be directly extrapolated to individual-level relationships.

The samples of 25–28 countries do not automatically represent all European countries. Differences in the availability of economic indicators result in variations in sample composition across relationships.

For Schwartz Human Values, the use of relative scores and the incompletely established cross-cultural comparability of the measurements require additional caution.

The project does not include independent tests of psychometric measurement invariance for the analyzed ESS Round 11 sample.

The LOO and temporal analyses describe the sensitivity of results to specific analytical changes. They do not constitute confidence intervals and do not eliminate the potential influence of unobserved factors.

The 36 relationships were examined exploratorily. Coefficient magnitude and agreement between two correlation methods should not be confused with confirmation of a hypothesis.

Aggregated ESS estimates are subject to sampling uncertainty, which is not fully propagated into the country-level correlation coefficients.

The reported correlations are not causal estimates and do not allow explanatory mechanisms to be identified without additional analyses and assumptions.

## 7. Summary of the Results

The results reveal relatively consistent descriptive associations between social trust and several socioeconomic indicators, particularly GDP PPS, employment, material deprivation, and income inequality.

For Schwartz Human Values, certain relationships involving employment, GDP PPS, and material deprivation exhibit coefficient signs that agree between Pearson and Spearman and remain unchanged across the examined sensitivity scenarios.

By contrast, relationships involving inflation and certain associations with unemployment exhibit small coefficients, substantial variation, or sensitivity to analytical specifications.

Overall, the findings provide an empirical basis for theoretical discussion and the formulation of additional research questions.

The results do not support causal conclusions, direct individual-level inferences, or automatic generalization to all European countries.
