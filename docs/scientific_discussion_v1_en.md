
# Scientific Discussion V1 — European Values, Ideological Self-Placement & Socioeconomic Development

**Status:** FINAL — Step 66

**Empirical basis:** ESS Round 11, Eurostat, Scientific Evidence Matrix V1

**Study design:** Cross-sectional, exploratory, country-level analysis

## 1. Objective of the Discussion

This study examined 36 associations between six aggregated variables from European Social Survey (ESS) Round 11 and six Eurostat socioeconomic indicators, using data available for 25–28 countries, depending on the relationship examined.

The results reveal certain descriptive patterns involving generalized social trust, Schwartz value priorities, ideological self-placement, and socioeconomic conditions.

The purpose of this discussion is to situate these findings within the existing scientific literature, identify alternative explanations, and clarify the interpretations permitted by the research design.

The analysis does not aim to demonstrate causal effects and does not constitute a confirmatory test of the theories discussed.

## 2. Social Trust and Socioeconomic Development

One of the central findings is the positive association between aggregated social trust and GDP per capita expressed as an index in purchasing power standards (GDP per capita in PPS, EU average = 100), alongside positive associations with employment and negative associations with material deprivation and income inequality.

The GDP PPS indicator allows comparisons of relative GDP per capita across countries while accounting for differences in price levels. It does not represent the economic growth rate or a direct measure of individual well-being.

These results are consistent with comparative research on social capital and economic performance.

Knack and Keefer (1997) identified relationships between social trust, income levels, and income distribution, highlighting the relevance of social capital for understanding economic differences between societies.

However, the literature does not assume a single explanatory direction.

Rothstein and Uslaner (2005) discuss the role of economic equality and equality of opportunity in the development of social trust. From this perspective, favorable socioeconomic conditions may contribute to higher levels of trust, rather than the relationship necessarily operating in the opposite direction.

Algan and Cahuc (2010) examine the relationship between culturally inherited trust and economic growth using an empirical strategy that differs from cross-sectional correlations. Their findings are relevant when discussing possible mechanisms, but they do not provide a causal interpretation of the associations identified in the present study.

The literature also emphasizes the importance of institutional quality, education, and sociocultural differences in explaining levels of social trust (Nannestad, 2008).

Consequently, the Social Trust–GDP PPS association identified in our analysis may reflect complex relationships between material conditions, institutions, and generalized trust.

The available data do not allow us to establish the direction of these relationships or isolate the independent contribution of each factor.

Furthermore, the descriptive stability of certain coefficients across Leave-One-Country-Out scenarios does not demonstrate that these relationships would remain unchanged in other populations, periods, or analytical specifications.

## 3. Schwartz Human Values and Socioeconomic Conditions

### 3.1. Theoretical Interpretation of Value Patterns

Schwartz's theory describes personal values through a circular motivational structure in which certain orientations are compatible, whereas others reflect opposing motivational goals (Schwartz, 2012).

In the present study, Openness to Change and Conservation exhibited associations with employment rates in opposite directions.

Similarly, Self-Transcendence and Self-Enhancement displayed opposing patterns of associations with GDP per capita in PPS and material deprivation.

These results are descriptively relevant because certain relationships remain consistent between Pearson and Spearman correlations and retain their direction across the examined sensitivity scenarios.

Nevertheless, they do not demonstrate that value priorities determine socioeconomic performance.

The distribution of aggregated values may also be associated with educational attainment, demographic structure, institutions, labor market organization, or historical differences between societies.

These alternative explanations were not independently tested in the present analysis.

Moreover, the motivational oppositions described by Schwartz's theory do not automatically imply that every observed aggregate-level correlation between a value dimension and an economic indicator must have the opposite sign of the corresponding correlation involving another value dimension.

### 3.2. The Importance of Relative Value Scores

Schwartz Human Values were calculated using scores centered around each respondent's mean response across the PVQ-21 items.

This procedure is intended to represent relative value priorities rather than absolute levels of value endorsement.

Consequently, the resulting dimensions are not mathematically independent. Opposing correlation patterns must also be interpreted in relation to this property of the measurement procedure.

Score centering may influence the statistical relationships observed between value dimensions, while comparisons of national means must be interpreted in terms of the relative priorities defined by the scoring procedure.

It is therefore not justified to automatically attribute distinct psychological mechanisms to each dimension solely on the basis of the sign of a correlation coefficient.

### 3.3. Cross-Country Comparability of Human Values

A central methodological limitation concerns the cross-cultural comparability of the measurement instrument.

Davidov, Schmidt, and Schwartz (2008) examined the adequacy of the ESS instrument for measuring human values across several European countries and emphasized the importance of evaluating measurement invariance in comparative research.

In particular, comparisons of latent construct means require appropriate forms of scalar invariance or methodologically justified alternatives when exact invariance is not satisfied.

Cieciuch et al. (2018) investigated the use of approximate measurement invariance for the PVQ-21, showing that the feasibility of comparing human values differs across dimensions and groups of countries.

In their study, approximate invariance was supported in almost all examined rounds for Openness to Change and Self-Enhancement, whereas for Self-Transcendence and Conservation, it was identified within subsets of countries.

More recently, Fischer and Karl (2025) examined the comparability of human values using 11 ESS rounds, 261 samples, and 374,565 respondents.

Their research provides support for the general theoretical structure while also identifying comparability difficulties in certain national samples and across time.

The authors report that evidence regarding invariance depends on the level and configuration of the comparisons, and that results obtained for particular samples or periods should not automatically be generalized.

These findings do not imply that the PVQ-21 is generally unsuitable for cross-country comparisons. Rather, they demonstrate that the degree of comparability should be evaluated for the relevant dimensions, countries, and periods.

The present study does not include independent tests of configural, metric, or scalar invariance for the analyzed ESS Round 11 sample.

The exclusion analyses involving Estonia and the United Kingdom assess the influence of these observations on aggregate correlations, but they do not test the psychometric equivalence of the instrument.

For this reason, Schwartz–Eurostat relationships must be considered exploratory findings conditional on the operationalization of human values and their comparability across countries.

## 4. Left–Right Ideological Self-Placement

Most associations between mean ideological self-placement and socioeconomic indicators exhibited relatively small correlation coefficients.

One potential interpretive limitation arises from the fact that the meaning of the Left–Right scale may vary across political contexts.

Otjes, Živković, and Katsanidou (2026) examine how Left–Right self-positioning reflects economic and cultural conflicts across different political and socioeconomic contexts.

Their research highlights the relevance of how political actors associate different issues with the ideological distinction between the Left and the Right.

Consequently, the same numerical position on the ideological scale should not automatically be interpreted as having an identical meaning across countries.

Furthermore, the use of national means may conceal important differences between social groups, polarized distributions, or distinct ideological orientations that produce the same average value.

For example, two countries may exhibit the same mean Left–Right position even though respondents in one country are concentrated near the center, whereas respondents in the other are more strongly distributed toward the extremes.

Therefore, the coefficients observed in the present study characterize only the relationships between the aggregated indicators used in the analysis.

They do not constitute evidence for the general relevance or irrelevance of ideology to socioeconomic phenomena.

## 5. Interpretation of the Sensitivity Analyses

### 5.1. Leave-One-Country-Out Analysis

The Leave-One-Country-Out (LOO) procedure allows us to examine the extent to which observed relationships depend on the presence of a particular country.

The preservation of the coefficient sign across all scenarios is a useful descriptive finding, but it does not demonstrate general statistical stability.

Some relationships exhibited changes in coefficient magnitude without sign reversals.

Other relationships, characterized by baseline coefficients close to zero, exhibited sign reversals following relatively small numerical changes.

Interpretation should therefore consider coefficient direction, initial magnitude, and variation simultaneously.

The ranges of coefficients obtained through LOO analysis are not confidence intervals and do not fully quantify inferential uncertainty.

Furthermore, LOO analysis is not equivalent to validating the results using an independent sample of countries.

### 5.2. Temporal Sensitivity

The analysis of alternative temporal specifications revealed important changes in certain associations, particularly those involving inflation.

These variations indicate that interpretation may be sensitive to the choice of socioeconomic reference year.

Inflation can fluctuate considerably between years, and examining its association with social indicators that may change less rapidly requires caution.

The results do not demonstrate an effect of inflation on human values or social trust. Instead, they show that certain cross-sectional associations depend on the temporal context selected for the economic indicator.

Where data availability permitted, temporal comparisons used common-country samples to distinguish changes in indicator values from changes in sample composition.

For relationships involving the Gini coefficient, moving from the main sample to the common-country sample also involved a reduction in the number of available countries.

This sample-composition effect must be distinguished from the change associated with the socioeconomic reference year.

### 5.3. Exclusion of Estonia (EE) and the United Kingdom (GB)

The EE/GB exclusion analyses identified no strict sign reversals across the 24 Schwartz relationships.

This finding indicates that the observed directions did not depend exclusively on the inclusion of these two countries, within the limits of the scenarios that were actually applicable.

However, the United Kingdom was included only in the four Schwartz baseline samples involving GDP PPS.

For the other five economic indicators, excluding the United Kingdom did not represent an actual change in sample composition.

Consequently, zero coefficient changes associated with excluding GB in these cases should not be interpreted as independent evidence of stability.

The stability observed in applicable scenarios does not justify generalization to arbitrary country exclusions or other ESS rounds.

Similarly, the absence of sign reversals does not imply the absence of changes in coefficient magnitude or demonstrate psychometric comparability.

## 6. Limitations and Future Research Directions

### 6.1. Ecological Inference

The analysis uses countries as the statistical units.

Robinson (1950) highlighted the difficulties involved in inferring individual behavior from correlations calculated between groups.

Therefore, associations between national averages of trust or human values and economic indicators do not demonstrate the existence of equivalent relationships at the individual level.

Moreover, an aggregate-level result may be influenced by the population composition of each country without reflecting an identical individual-level association.

### 6.2. Sample Coverage and Generalizability

The number of countries differs across relationships depending on the availability of Eurostat indicators.

The analyzed sample should not automatically be treated as representative of all European countries.

Furthermore, the analysis includes 36 exploratory comparisons, which requires caution when selecting findings for discussion.

Relationships that appear descriptively stronger should not automatically be treated as confirmed hypotheses.

The magnitude of a correlation alone is insufficient to support a general conclusion.

Pearson–Spearman agreement and sign preservation across sensitivity analyses support the descriptive characterization of the findings, but they do not substitute for an explicit inferential strategy.

### 6.3. Estimation Uncertainty and Unobserved Factors

Aggregated ESS estimates are derived from survey data and are subject to their own sampling uncertainty.

The country-level correlations do not fully propagate this uncertainty.

Furthermore, the study does not isolate the effects of potentially relevant variables such as education, institutions, demographic composition, or economic structure.

These limitations prevent the attribution of a single explanation to the observed associations.

The sensitivity analyses improve the transparency of the results assessment, but they do not eliminate problems associated with confounding, sample selection, or measurement error.

### 6.4. Future Research

Relevant future research directions include explicitly evaluating PVQ-21 measurement invariance for the analyzed sample, examining relationships through multilevel models, extending the analysis to multiple ESS rounds, and investigating associations adjusted for theoretically selected contextual factors.

A longitudinal extension could allow the examination of changes over time and help distinguish certain persistent differences between countries from temporal changes in the indicators.

Additionally, analyzing the individual-level distributions of ideological self-placement could provide information beyond that obtained from national averages alone.

Multilevel models could allow the simultaneous analysis of individual and contextual characteristics, provided that the data structure and research assumptions are appropriately specified.

Such extensions would require additional assumptions, specific methodological evaluations, and an appropriate analytical architecture.

They are not treated as completed findings of V1.

## 7. Conclusion of the Discussion

The study identifies descriptive associations between social trust, value priorities, ideological self-placement, and socioeconomic conditions within the analyzed sample.

Some observed relationships are consistent with existing comparative research, particularly regarding social trust and socioeconomic development.

In the case of Schwartz Human Values and Left–Right self-placement, interpretation requires additional caution because of measurement-specific characteristics and cross-country comparability issues.

The main contribution of V1 is the reproducibility-oriented integration of ESS–Eurostat data and the systematic assessment of the sensitivity of the observed associations.

The findings provide a foundation for future research without establishing causal relationships or definitive explanatory mechanisms.
